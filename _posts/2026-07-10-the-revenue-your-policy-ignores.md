A community bank loses its core platform for five days. No wires, no ACH, no online banking, no originations. It files a business interruption claim on the cyber policy and recovers $48,000, against a five-day revenue figure well north of $200,000.

That comparison is the intuitive one, and it is wrong for a reason that is specific to how a bank earns money.

## Interest income does not stop

A loan accrues interest under its note. A deposit accrues interest expense under its terms. Neither is conditioned on whether the servicing platform is up, so when the core comes back the accruals are sitting there. Multiplying net interest income by outage days produces a large number that is not a loss.

What stops is fee income. Overdraft and NSF charges, interchange, wire fees, service charges, ATM fees. Those are earned per transaction, and a transaction that does not happen does not come back. That is your realistic BI claim, and most BI definitions cover it. Read the opening scenario again with that in mind and the $48,000 may have covered most of what was actually lost.

## Where the exclusion does bite

Run the clock out to two or three weeks and it changes. Depositors who cannot reach their money move it, and replacing that funding costs more on a loan book you cannot reprice at the same speed. Borrowers under stress stop paying, loans migrate toward non-accrual, and booked interest gets reversed.

Both are interest income losses in the plain sense, and both are what the exclusion bars. The gap is real. The market just has it priced backwards, overstated for the outage every tabletop models and understated for the event almost nobody models.

## Three ways it shows up in the form

**Explicit exclusion.** The income definition carves out "interest or investment income" by name.

**Ambiguous definition.** The form says "income from business operations" or "net profit before income taxes" and never mentions interest income, leaving the largest line on your income statement to be characterized by a carrier at claim time.

**Flat hourly rate.** Some programs pay a set rate per hour. One I reviewed paid $500. A flat rate does not ask what you lost, so it can overpay a short outage and badly underpay a long one, and you cannot tell which until the claim is open.

Most banks I reviewed had one of these three. One had solved it.

## The fix

Some carriers write an endorsement deleting the interest and investment income exclusion from the income definition. Where I have seen it, it cost between nothing and $2,000 a year. If your carrier does not offer one, ask in writing whether "income from business operations" includes net interest income for a financial institution, and keep the answer. An ambiguity resolved at renewal is worth more than an argument you win eighteen months into a claim.

Your cyber policy was written for businesses that stop earning when their systems stop. A bank does not work that way, and the BI definition was never adjusted for it. In a short outage that mismatch is mostly harmless. In the event that threatens the institution, it is the difference between a covered loss and an argument.
