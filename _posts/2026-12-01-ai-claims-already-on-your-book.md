Many casualty carriers have been filing AI-related exclusions: Block what you can't measure -- that response makes sense, if AI liability is a new product category. It makes less sense if the exposure is already on your book.

A lawyer uses an AI research tool and submits a brief with fabricated citations. That's a legal malpractice claim. Over half of legal professional liability insurers surveyed by EPIC earlier this year reported AI-related claims increasing -- the first time the survey has shown that. A health insurer's algorithm denies coverage at scale and triggers an AG investigation. That lands as an E&O and regulatory loss. An employer's AI screening tool rejects hundreds of candidates based on characteristics it was never told to consider. The EEOC complaint goes into the EPL book.

None of these claims wait for a new insurance product. They are already arriving through your professional liability, your E&O, your D&O, your EPL, and your cyber policies you have written for decades. Whether the policy responds, and how much you have to pay, will be fought claim by claim. It will be messy, but the exposure enters your existing book regardless.

Allianz's [report on AI losses](https://commercial.allianz.com/news-and-insights/reports/artificial-intelligence.html) in November called it the "tip of the iceberg": it confirmed AI claims are frequently underreported, difficult to quantify or attribute, and recorded under traditional categories like cyber, D&O, or product liability without the AI element being flagged. Based on current claims data alone, AI would not rank in the top 20 loss drivers. That says more about the labels than about the losses. Few if any carriers have AI-specific claims coding in their systems. The claims are already there, but invisible, as the labels are just missing. And they will grow -- quickly.

The carriers excluding standalone AI liability are not safe -- they are excluding a product that barely exists. The exposure they already carry is the one nobody has priced.

## Where measurement pressure comes from

After the 2020-21 ransomware wave, reinsurers tightened treaty conditions for cyber and started requiring external risk scores. That demand created a market for firms like BitSight and SecurityScorecard almost overnight. To be clear, these scores didn't create a new cyber insurance product. Instead, they gave carriers a way to measure cyber risk in policies they were already writing.

AI risk could follow a similar path: forcing carriers to see what's already in their portfolio.

My bet is that the first meaningful pressure to measure AI risk will come from reinsurers. Reinsurers see aggregate exposure across books. AI-related claims will show up as a frequency shift before the market agrees on a label for them.

When reinsurers start asking what share of the portfolio involves AI-augmented decisions, and that question becomes a treaty condition, every carrier and MGA has to find an answer.

## The public record is richer than the market assumes

The pushback I frequently hear: AI risk can't be measured from the inside because companies won't share their model architectures or governance practices with insurers. And it can't be measured from the outside because the decisions happen inside a black box. That's a fair criticism of the technology. But the underwriting question is actually quite different: which companies have a gap between their AI exposure and their governance of that exposure?

And the evidence is already public. In a survey of AI decision-makers in September, EY found that 89% of large public companies encountered AI-related risks, yet 47% admitted skipping their own governance processes for urgent deployments. SEC filings add a harder layer. Proxy statements reveal board-level AI oversight structures and committee assignments. These carry securities law liability, so they're not just voluntary surveys.

Litigation adds another layer. Close to [400 AI-related lawsuits](https://ailawsuittracker.com) are tracked, and the docket is accelerating. In D&O specifically, AI-related securities class actions made up 13% of filings in the first half of 2026 but represented 73% of Disclosure Dollar Loss -- the total drop in market capitalization alleged by plaintiffs. These are not insured losses. But they are the clearest signal of where severity is concentrating. FTC enforcement and state attorneys general investigations are building a parallel record. And regulatory geography matters -- a company operating in Illinois faces structurally different AI exposure than one in Texas, regardless of its internal governance.

You can group these signals into four dimensions: (1) How consequential are the AI-assisted decisions a company makes? (2) How much regulatory scrutiny do those decisions attract? (3) What does the litigation and enforcement record show? (4) How does the company's governance compare to its exposure level?

Each signal alone is incomplete. But the hypothesis is that the combination of these signals differentiates companies on the dimension that matters -- AI exposure relative to AI governance.

## What remains unproven

My hypothesis is that external signals can identify AI exposure and AI governance gaps. They can't yet establish the frequency or severity of an insured loss. Until someone matches the observable signals against actual claims experience, every pricing approach is informed speculation.

Casualty carriers already have this data sitting in their claims files. What they lack is a framework for identifying which claims involve AI-augmented decisions and measuring the increment. The search terms exist: algorithmic, automated decision, AI-assisted screening, model output. That query isn't being run systematically.

The insurance industry has solved this kind of problem before. Natural catastrophe modeling firms built the foundation for property pricing by pooling loss data no single carrier would share on its own. The firm that builds the first credible link between observable AI risk signals and actual loss experience sets the pricing reference. And the reinsurer asking the question will determine how fast it happens.
