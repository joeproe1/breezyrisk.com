Many carriers have been filing AI-related exclusions: Block what you can't measure. That response makes sense if AI liability is a new product category. It makes less sense if the exposure is already on your book.

Look at where the claims are landing.

A lawyer uses an AI research tool and submits a brief with fabricated citations. That's a legal malpractice claim. Over half of legal professional liability insurers surveyed by EPIC earlier this year reported AI-related claims increasing -- the first time the survey has shown that. A health insurer's algorithm denies coverage at scale and triggers an AG investigation. That lands as an E&O and regulatory loss. An employer's AI screening tool rejects hundreds of candidates based on characteristics it was never told to consider. The EEOC complaint goes into the EPL book.

None of these claims wait for a new insurance product. They already arrive through your professional liability, E&O, D&O, and EPL policies you have written for decades. Whether the policy responds, and how much the carrier pays, will be fought claim by claim. It will be messy, but the exposure enters the existing book regardless.

Allianz's [report on AI losses](https://commercial.allianz.com/news-and-insights/reports/artificial-intelligence.html) in November called it the "tip of the iceberg": confirmed AI claims are frequently underreported, difficult to quantify or attribute, and recorded under traditional categories like cyber, D&O, or product liability without the AI element being flagged. Based on current claims data alone, AI would not rank in the top 20 loss drivers. Few if any carriers have AI-specific claims coding in their systems. But the claims are already there, the labels are just missing.

The carriers excluding standalone AI liability are excluding a product that barely exists. The exposure they already carry is the one nobody has priced.

## Where measurement pressure comes from

After the 2020-21 ransomware wave, reinsurers tightened treaty conditions for cyber and started requiring external risk scores. That demand created a market for firms like BitSight almost overnight. To be clear, these scores didn't create a new cyber insurance product. Instead, they gave carriers a way to measure cyber risk in policies they were already writing.

AI risk could follow a similar path: forcing carriers to see what's already in their portfolio.

My bet is that the first meaningful pressure to measure AI risk will come from reinsurers. They see aggregate exposure across books. AI-related claims will show up as a frequency shift in bordereaux before the market agrees on a label for them.

When reinsurers start asking what share of the portfolio involves AI-augmented decisions, and that question becomes a treaty condition, every carrier and MGA has to find an answer.

## The public record is richer than the market assumes

The pushback I hear often: AI risk can't be measured from the inside because companies won't share their model architectures or governance practices. And it can't be measured from the outside because the decisions happen inside a black box. That's true for the technology. But the underwriting question is actually quite different: which companies have a gap between their AI exposure and their governance of that exposure?

And the evidence is already public. In a survey of AI decision-makers in September, EY found that 89% of large public companies encountered AI-related risks, yet 47% admitted skipping their own governance processes for urgent deployments. SEC filings add a harder layer. Proxy statements reveal board-level AI oversight structures and committee assignments. These carry securities law liability, so they're not just voluntary surveys.

Litigation adds a second layer. Close to [400 AI-related lawsuits](https://ailawsuittracker.com) are tracked, and the docket is accelerating. In D&O specifically, AI-related securities class actions made up 13% of filings in the first half of 2026 but represented 73% of Disclosure Dollar Loss -- the total drop in market capitalization alleged by plaintiffs. These are not insured losses. They are the clearest signal of where severity is concentrating. FTC enforcement and state attorneys general investigations are building a parallel record. And regulatory geography matters -- a company operating in Illinois faces structurally different AI exposure than one in Texas, regardless of its internal governance.

You can group these signals into a few dimensions, such as: how consequential are the AI-assisted decisions a company makes? How much regulatory scrutiny do those decisions attract? What does the litigation and enforcement record show? And how does the company's governance compare to its exposure level? Each signal alone is incomplete. But can the combination of these signals differentiate companies on the dimension that matters, exposure relative to governance? A carrier using AI to triage claims with no board-level AI oversight is quite a different risk than one with the same deployment and a documented governance program.

## What remains unproven

External signals can identify exposure and governance gaps. They can't yet establish the frequency or severity of an insured loss. Until someone matches observable signals against actual claims experience, every pricing approach is informed speculation.

Professional liability and E&O carriers already have this data sitting in their claims files. What they lack is a framework for identifying which claims involve AI-augmented decisions and measuring the increment. The search terms exist: algorithmic, automated decision, AI-assisted screening, model output. That query isn't being run systematically.

The insurance industry has solved this kind of problem before. Catastrophe modeling firms built the foundation for property pricing by pooling loss data no single carrier would share on its own. The firm that builds the first credible link between observable AI risk signals and actual loss experience sets the pricing reference. And the reinsurer asking the question will determine how fast it happens.
